# Why Pursue Spirituality?

Spirituality is an umbrella term to understand reality as a whole, including both what is eternal and unchanging, and everything that arises from it and is comparatively temporary or changing. In this view, the Divine is the fullest and most fundamental reality. Everything else derives from it and reflects it only partially[^1]. The world we experience is therefore real, but in a more limited and imperfect way than the Divine reality from which it derives.

With this viewpoint, in one sense, the question “Why pursue spirituality?” may seem misplaced. If spirituality concerns what is most fundamental, then its pursuit cannot be justified solely by appealing to something less fundamental. To understand the source of our existence is already to approach the question of its purpose.

In fact, the very desire to pursue this understanding and the ultimate bliss it brings may itself be seen as a blessing, an expression of divine grace. More generally, the alignment of human intelligence with divine intelligence, to whatever extent it occurs, is possible only through divine grace. For someone already inclined to pursue spirituality, the answer may therefore be simple: because the possibility has been given to us, and because pursuing it fulfills the purpose of our existence.

Yet these considerations may speak most readily to those who already feel drawn to this path. Our aim here is to offer a brief account of the questions spirituality addresses for people of our time, with their varied interests and concerns. We hope this will provide a starting point for those who wish to understand the path and continue along it.

In a certain sense, spirituality can be seen as addressing the problems of the world at their root. This does not mean that it replaces the particular knowledge needed to address a political, economic, or technological problem. Rather, it concerns the understanding of reality that should guide these pursuits and determine their proper ends. As long as attention remains confined to the changing world and to the competing interests of separate individuals and groups, divisions and conflicts cannot be completely overcome. An enduring unity must be sought in what we have called the spiritual realm. By this, we do not mean merely something inward, invisible, or emotional, but that which relates us to the Divine reality on which all these differences depend.

Politics, economics, technology, psychology, and history can illuminate particular conditions, but they cannot, by themselves, provide the ultimate basis by which human purposes should be judged. When treated as sufficient in themselves, they may reproduce or aggravate the disorder they seek to remedy. Indeed, such efforts may only make conditions progressively worse, like a person sinking in quicksand: the more they struggle, the more they sink.

The guiding principle of pursuing spirituality is therefore to seek the truth and accept what it requires of us, trusting in the Divine rather than making our allegiance to truth conditional on the consequences we expect. This expresses an order of priority: our worldly concerns are to find their place only within a life directed towards the Divine, rather than spiritual life becoming a means of satisfying worldly concerns[^2].

The same principle applies to knowledge: the knowledge needed to conduct human affairs, however valuable, does not exhaust the wisdom by which those affairs should ultimately be guided[^3]. In other words, the divine is the absolute reality, whereas the world has only a relative reality, deriving its existence from the divine rather than existing independently of it, just as the reflection of the sun in water has no existence independent of the sun. Similarly, the world is not unreal in itself, but becomes illusory when viewed in isolation from the Divine reality on which it depends[^4].

Our task as individuals is therefore not to bring the whole course of events under our control, but to seek the truth and bring our understanding and actions into conformity with it. This does not require indifference to injustice or the abandonment of our responsibilities. Efforts to seek justice may continue, but with the recognition that our understanding is limited and that our own judgments must not be mistaken for an infallible expression of Divine Will. We remain responsible for how we act, while acknowledging that the final course of events is not ours to command[^5].

From this perspective, even familiar words such as freedom, equality, progress, and intellect acquire a different significance. Freedom is not merely the ability to satisfy our desires, but freedom from subjection to them. Equality finds its deeper meaning in the recognition that all beings arise from the same Divine reality. Progress is not simply an increase in possessions, comforts, or powers, but an advance towards spiritual understanding and liberation. And intellect, in its fullest sense, extends beyond the mere ability to reason.

One may still ask: why is truth worth pursuing if it is not intended to increase one's happiness[^6], well-being, fulfillment, or some other good that is already valued? The answer begins from the opposite ordering: individual goods are to be judged relative to truth, not truth relative to individual goods. We seek the truth because it is true. This sounds circular only if we assume that every pursuit must be justified by some further benefit. But not everything can be justified by appealing to some further benefit; ultimately, something must be valued for its own sake: something must be sought for its own sake rather than as a means to something else. From the spiritual viewpoint, truth occupies this place. Happiness, usefulness, and fulfillment are not denied, but neither are they made the standards by which reality is judged. The proper function of the intellect is to understand reality as it is, independently of our preferences. Truth is not a means to an end. Truth is the end against which other ends are judged.

From this viewpoint, the highest aim of human life is to attain knowledge of ultimate reality, or at least to advance towards it. Such knowledge is all-encompassing, not in the sense of accumulating every possible fact about the world, but because it concerns the source and principles on which all particular truths depend. Knowledge of natural phenomena belongs within this larger order; it does not stand outside it.

Knowledge of nature may nevertheless become a support for spiritual understanding when natural forms and relationships are understood as reflections of truths that extend beyond them. In this sense, it may serve as a ladder. But the value of such a ladder lies in what it leads towards, not merely in its usefulness for describing or manipulating the physical world. Nor does every resemblance between a scientific theory and a spiritual teaching establish that they express the same knowledge. The authority of sacred teachings is understood within this perspective to derive from the reality they express, not from their agreement with a scientific theory. To make that authority depend on a changing account of nature would reverse the very order under discussion. Thus, however extensive our knowledge of the physical universe becomes, the spiritual perspective does not identify the physical universe with the whole of reality.

The same commitment to truth must govern spiritual inquiry itself. Our preferences and existing beliefs cannot determine what is true. The experiences on which they are based may provide starting points for inquiry, but our interpretations of those experiences remain open to correction. Indeed, our attachments may become particularly strong impediments here, because spirituality concerns not only what we think, but how we ought to live. An inquiry into it may require us to reconsider desires, habits, and ambitions that we would prefer to leave undisturbed. The tension between what we wish to be true and what an honest inquiry requires us to acknowledge is therefore not incidental to the spiritual path.

How, then, might we begin? Most of us did not begin studying science or math by attempting to reconstruct it entirely for ourselves. We learned from teachers and textbooks, and gradually deepened our understanding through reasoning, observation, and experiment. Spiritual inquiry can likewise begin by approaching established teachings and qualified teachers. Such trust requires discernment, and it is a beginning rather than a substitute for understanding.

A reader may nevertheless say: all this is well and good, but please leave me to the pursuits I am passionate about, whether my vocation or other interests; these are enough for me. Whatever our vocation or interests, there is something fundamental to all of them: we experience them. Even now, as you read these words, you are experiencing the act of reading. There is not only an activity that can be described from outside, but also the fact that this activity is experienced. Even a complete physical or computational description of what happens in your mind and body as you read these words would not, by itself, explain why there is an experience of reading at all. This is not to rule out a future scientific explanation of consciousness, or to claim that this question establishes the truth of any spiritual doctrine. Rather, it is to recognize that understanding reality also requires understanding the nature of conscious experience itself, a question that lies at the heart of spiritual inquiry.

Spiritual inquiry, as understood thus, asks not only how our experiences arise and change, but what it is that knows them and how this knowledge relates to ultimate reality. It does not stop at cataloguing mental states or producing unusual experiences. Whatever our vocation or interests, they are inseparable from the fact that we experience them. Thus, even if we are content to devote our lives to these pursuits, there remains the more fundamental question of the nature of the consciousness through which we experience them. This is itself a central concern of spirituality.

Having considered the relevance of spirituality to individual pursuits, we now return to its broader significance for human societies and civilizations, beginning with the relationship between spirituality and traditional religion. Religion is not exhausted by the outward observances, institutional affiliations, or professions of belief with which it is often identified. Its teachings and practices are intended to relate human life to a reality beyond the individual and to provide means by which that relationship may be understood and lived. The difficulty is not the existence of rites or authoritative teachings, but their reduction to external conformity without an understanding of what they express. Conversely, spiritual understanding should not be confused with an invention assembled from whatever beliefs and practices happen to appeal to us.

Understood in this way, genuine religion offers a way beyond two opposing extremes: rigid adherence to rituals and dogmas on the one hand, and the outright rejection of spiritual truth on the other. It offers a way beyond that opposition by preserving the connection between outward forms and their inward meaning. When that connection is forgotten, religion may become an attachment to forms as ends in themselves. When the forms are rejected without any understanding of what they preserve, the possibility of a deeper knowledge may be rejected along with them.

For those who categorically reject religion, the danger is not that they must inevitably become opportunists or nihilists. Rather, the danger arises when it leads to the rejection of any basis for truth and goodness beyond individual or collective preferences. Where truth is accepted only when convenient, inquiry gives way to opportunism. Where every purpose is treated as arbitrary, the possibility of a purpose worth serving is itself called into question. These dangers are not overcome merely by professing a religion. Religious beliefs too can be made instruments of convenience, ambition, or self-justification.

What matters then is not merely the beliefs one holds, but whether one is genuinely committed to seeking the truth. This commitment lies at the heart of spirituality, which asks us to seek what is true because it is true, rather than subordinating it to our interests, and to allow our purposes and conduct to be corrected by what we come to understand. Its promise is not that reality will accommodate our preferences, but that we may cease to mistake our preferences for the measure of reality.

## Notes

[^1]: Leibniz in *Monadology* describes God as the absolutely perfect and original substance from which all created beings derive their existence, with their perfections being finite reflections of divine attributes (§§41–48). He further describes each monad as a living mirror of the same universe, representing it from its own limited perspective, much as a single city appears differently when viewed from different directions (§§56–57). Finally, he distinguishes rational minds as images of the divine itself, capable of understanding the order of the universe and consciously imitating something of it (§83).

[^2]: In Matthew 6:33 we have: *“But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you”*

[^3]: In Sima Qian’s *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*, Chapter 63), Confucius visits Lao Tzu to inquire about rites, ceremonies, etiquette, and the conventions of proper social conduct. Lao Tzu instead advises him to abandon pride, excessive desires, and ambition. Confucius subsequently compares Lao Tzu to a dragon whose movements he cannot fathom.

[^4]: In *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* (verse 20), Ādi Śaṅkarācārya writes: *Brahma satyaṃ jagan mithyā.* A literal translation is roughly: “Brahman alone is real (unchanging and eternal); the world is not ultimately real (being transient and subject to change)”.

[^5]: In *Bhagavad Gītā* (2.47) we have: *karmanyevādhikāraste mā phaleșu kadācana &#124; mā karmaphalaheturbhūrmā te sango‘stuakarmaņi*. A literal translation is roughly: “You have the right to action alone, never to its fruits. Do not make the fruits of action your motive, and do not cling to inaction”.

[^6]: Of course, as presented in the beginning of this essay, one may simply say, if our understanding of truth does not lead to a deeper sense of joy, it may be that our understanding of either truth or happiness remains incomplete. Note that happiness need not be understood as merely material comfort or the fulfillment of our existing desires. In that spirit, in *Analects* (6.11), Confucius praises his disciple Yan Hui: “With a single bowl of food and a single gourd of water, living in a humble lane—others could not bear such hardship, yet Hui did not lose his joy”.
